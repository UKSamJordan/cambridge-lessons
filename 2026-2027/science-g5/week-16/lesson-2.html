🌍 Real-World Science: Ships, Plimsoll Lines & Fair Testing

🚢

Cargo Ships & Plimsoll Lines

Every giant cargo ship has a Plimsoll Line painted on its hull. If too much cargo is loaded, the ship sits too low and water can flood over the rim!

📐

Marine Engineers Testing Hulls

Before building a real ship, engineers test scale models in water tanks. They change one hull shape feature at a time so it is a fair test.

🔎

Spotting Faulty Data

When a scatter graph shows one strange dot far off the pattern, scientists never ignore it or bend the line — they circle the anomalous result and repeat that trial!

📋 Steps to Success & Safety

  1. Use the exact same 60 g lump of clay for every boat width.
  2. Measure the hull width (cm) with a ruler before floating.
  3. Place paperclips gently in the centre, one by one.
  4. Count only the cargo held before the boat sinks.
  5. Plot points carefully: across to X, then up to Y.
⚠️ Classroom Water Safety
Wipe up spilled water immediately with paper towels so nobody slips. Keep clay off the floor!

🔬 Scientific Mechanism Explainer: Why Does a Wider Hull Hold More Cargo?

Ask pupils to explain first, then click to reveal the science!

1. Bigger Surface Area & Water Pushed Aside

Reshaping the same 60 g of clay into a wider hollow hull spreads the clay over a larger surface area, pushing more water aside.

2. Bigger Maximum Upthrust

As paperclips are added, Weight increases and the boat rides lower, so Upthrust increases to match Weight — up to a higher maximum for wider hulls!

3. Why It Finally Sinks (or Leaks!)

When Weight becomes greater than the maximum Upthrust (or water pours over a low rim / through a pinhole leak), forces become unbalanced and the boat sinks.
